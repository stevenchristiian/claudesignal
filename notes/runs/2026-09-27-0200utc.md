### The method test, and it cuts both ways

| Series | Floor | Blanks (Average) | Weighted predict | Doubling | Actual | **Weighted err** | Doubling err |
|--------|-------|------------------|------------------|----------|--------|------------------|--------------|
| BTC | +$37.5M | IBIT $96.1M | **$133.6M** | $75.0M | **+$134.5M** | **-0.7%** | -44.2% |
| ETH | +$4.7M | ETHA $24.2M, ETHB $6.2M | **$35.1M** | $9.4M | **+$87.0M** | **-59.7%** | -89.2% |

**The weighted rule beat doubling on both series for a second consecutive run — and its own error range on
a single day ran from 0.7% to 59.7%.** Yesterday's run recorded the rule as a "central estimate, not a
bound" on the strength of a 35% understatement. That was right, and today makes it unmissable.

**Why the spread, and it is the same diagnosis one level down.** The weighted rule replaced a flat
multiplier on the row with a per-fund average — but a *fund's own* print is itself a multiple of its average:

| Fund | Average | Printed | Multiple |
|------|---------|---------|----------|
| IBIT | $96.1M | $97.0M | **1.01x** |
| ETHA | $24.2M | $50.4M | **2.08x** |
| ETHB | $6.2M | $31.9M | **5.15x** |

So the BTC prediction was near-exact because its single blank fund printed almost exactly its average, and
the ETH prediction was 60% low because both of its blanks printed multiples of theirs — ETHB most of all,
and ETHB is the *small* fund, where an average is least stable.

> **Publishable refinement: the weighted estimate is most trustworthy when the blank is one large fund with
> a stable average, and least trustworthy when the blanks are several, or small.** A $6.2M average carries
> far less information about tomorrow than a $96.1M one. Do not quote a weighted figure without saying which
> case it is.
